# The University AI Harness
## Architecture, rationale, and what it gets us

*Prepared by Todd A. Whittaker and Ryan Brainerd · Draft for review · August 2026*

**How to read this document.** Every section opens with a plain-English paragraph that states what the thing is and why it matters. Technical depth follows. A reader who takes only the opening paragraphs will get the full argument; a reader who wants the engineering reasoning will find it immediately beneath. This structure is deliberate: the document is also the source material for an executive explainer, where the opening paragraphs become the visible surface and the depth becomes drill-down content.

---

## 1. The problem and the thesis

Franklin runs on capable systems that do not talk to each other, and on people who spend their scarce time being the integration layer. An advisor who wants to know which advisees have gone quiet must check Canvas, then Colleague, then their own notes. A chair comparing a program's outcomes against the labor market must pull IPEDS data, BLS projections, and enrollment reports from three places and reconcile them by hand. Institutional Research answers the same questions repeatedly because the answers live in systems most people cannot query. The information exists; the connective tissue does not.

The thesis of this project is that the connective tissue should be a single, institution-owned AI layer — a harness — that sits above our systems, answers questions in plain language, and enforces our access rules at every step. One harness, many doors: a chat surface, an advisor view, a reporting surface, and programmatic access for analysts, all drawing on the same governed connections to Canvas, Colleague, Kuali, our IPEDS benchmarking tool, federal labor data, and job-market signals. People ask questions in the role they hold and receive answers scoped to that role, with every question and answer logged.

The architecture described here has one organizing principle: **the user's own identity travels with every request, end to end, and authorization is checked independently at three separate layers.** The AI never acts as an all-seeing service account. Whatever the model does, it does as the specific advisor, chair, or dean who asked — and no more. This principle is what makes the rest of the design trustworthy, and it is the part that cannot be retrofitted later.

## 2. What this gets us

The value of this system is not "AI capability." It is specific questions that people at Franklin currently cannot answer, or can answer only through days of manual work, answered in seconds through infrastructure we govern. One example per constituency:

**An advisor** asks, "Which of my advisees haven't logged into Canvas in the past ten days, and how are they doing in their current courses?" Today this requires checking two systems student by student, so in practice it doesn't happen until a problem surfaces on its own. With the harness, it is one question, answered only for the students that advisor is assigned — the access rules see to that.

**A program chair** asks, "How did midterm performance in our capstone compare across the last four terms, and did the course revision change anything?" Today this is a request to IR with a queue behind it. With the harness, it is a self-service question against governed data, using the same definitions IR would use.

**A dean** asks, "Which of our programs feed the ten fastest-growing occupations in central Ohio, and where are our completion numbers relative to peer institutions?" Today, no one asks this, because answering it means joining our program data to BLS projections and IPEDS benchmarks by hand. With the harness, it is a single question spanning three sources — the kind of synthesis no vendor product will do for us, because no vendor federates our career-connectedness data with our institutional data.

**Career services** asks, "What skills appear most often in job postings for the roles our MSIS graduates take, and where do our course outcomes not cover them?" This connects postings data to curriculum data in Kuali — again, a join that exists nowhere today.

**An instructional designer** working in CourseForge, our internally built AI course-authoring tool, asks, "Which skills appear in postings for this program's target roles that our current course outcomes don't cover — and draft activities that would address them." Today CourseForge designs from the designer's knowledge and the AI's general training. Connected to the harness, it designs from those plus the institution's evidence: labor-market data, curriculum outcomes in Kuali, and what has actually engaged students in comparable courses.

**An analyst** connects directly to the data lake with SQL and sees exactly what their role permits, because the access rules live in the data platform itself, not in any one application. Power users get power tools without a side door around governance.

The pattern across all five: the harness does not create new data. It makes existing data answerable, by the right people, within the rules, with a record of who asked.

## 3. What this is not

Scope anxiety kills projects like this more often than technical failure does, so the boundaries deserve to be stated as plainly as the vision.

This is not a replacement for Colleague, Canvas, or any system of record. Those systems keep doing what they do; the harness reads from them and, much later and narrowly, may write to them through supervised channels. This is not a public-facing chatbot; every user is an authenticated Franklin employee with a defined role. This is not a surveillance tool; the same questions faculty and staff can ask today through slower means become faster, and nothing beyond that becomes visible — the access rules that govern people govern the AI acting on their behalf. Nothing in this system takes automatic action on a student record; in the eventual read-write phase, the AI proposes, a human reviews and edits, and only the human enacts. And this is not a bet on any single AI vendor; the model layer is deliberately interchangeable, and the durable assets — the data definitions, the tool contracts, the access model, the audit trail — are ours regardless of whose model answers on a given day.

## 4. The architecture in one pass

The system has five layers, drawn top to bottom in the companion diagram, with a set of services that apply everywhere. A request enters at the top and returns up the same path.

**Surfaces** are the ways in: a chat interface, an advisor workspace, a reporting view, programmatic API access, direct SQL for analysts, and existing applications — beginning with CourseForge, our AI course-authoring tool — connecting as clients. Surfaces we build new are deliberately thin, and none of them contains business logic or access decisions; existing applications keep their own interfaces and draw on the harness for what sits beneath.

**The front door** authenticates the person through our existing Microsoft sign-in, attaches their roles, and uses a small, inexpensive AI model to triage the request: classify what is being asked, decline what is out of scope, and select which larger model should do the real work. Input screening for hostile or malformed requests happens here.

**The orchestration layer** is where the selected model works: planning steps, choosing among the tools the person's role permits, and drawing on a semantic layer where terms like "enrolled student" and "retention" are defined exactly once, aligned with our IPEDS reporting definitions.

**The tool layer** exposes each connected system — Canvas, Colleague, Kuali, the IPEDS Oracle, BLS/O*NET, job postings — as an independent server speaking the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard for connecting AI systems to tools. Each server re-validates the user's identity against its own access rules. The harness is a client of these servers, never a superuser over them.

**The data layer** is a governed lake in our Azure environment, fed by scheduled extracts from institutional systems, with row- and column-level security bound to the same identity that entered at the front door. Analysts who connect directly hit the same rules as the AI.

Three of these layers — the front door, the tool servers, and the data platform — independently enforce authorization. This is labeled "PEP" (policy enforcement point) in the diagram. If any single layer is misconfigured or deceived, the other two still hold. The user's identity token, shown as the continuous thread down the left of the diagram, is what all three check.

Alongside all of this run the cross-cutting services: the identity provider as the single source of truth for roles; an append-only audit log recording who asked what, when, and under which role; output safeguards that suppress small-cell results and require answers to cite their sources; an evaluation suite that re-tests the system against known question-answer pairs whenever a model or schema changes; and, in the later read-write phase, a human approval workflow for any action that changes a record.

## 5. The layers in detail

### 5.1 Surfaces

The surfaces are thin by design: they render, they collect input, and they hold nothing that matters. This is what makes "one harness, many doors" honest. Because every access decision lives below the surface layer, adding a new surface — or letting a power user point a standards-compliant client at our tool servers — adds no new risk. Any client, ours or not, gets identical enforcement.

*Depth.* The chat surface ships first because it exercises the entire pipeline at the lowest build cost. The advisor workspace and reporting views come later, composed over the same tool layer, and only once enough tools exist beneath them to justify the build. The reporting surface should lean on Power BI against the lake's semantic models rather than reinventing visualization; the harness's contribution there is natural-language authoring and cross-source questions, not chart rendering. Analyst SQL access goes through the lake's SQL endpoints with the same Entra identity — no separate credentials, no separate rules.

CourseForge is the first existing application to take a place among the surfaces, and it matters to the strategy out of proportion to its size. Built internally by our instructional design group, it already supports bi-directional, ID-led course design with AI personas and pushes initial courses to Canvas — which makes it proof of institutional appetite and a day-one customer for the platform. It remains its own product with its own owners and roadmap; what changes is what sits beneath it, in stages. Its model calls move to the institutional model plane, inheriting governance, billing, and evaluation discipline with no functional change. Its Canvas push moves behind the Canvas server's write tools, giving the institution its first hardened write path on deliberately low-stakes content. And as sources come online, its design personas gain access to labor-market, curriculum, and course-performance data that no standalone, prompt-engineered tool could reach. CourseForge gains an institution behind it; the harness gains a customer that already exists.

### 5.2 The front door: identity, triage, screening

Everyone enters through the sign-in Franklin already uses. That sign-in produces a short-lived token naming the person and their roles, and that token — not a shared system account — is what every downstream layer sees. A small, fast AI model then performs triage: understanding what kind of question this is, declining early and politely when the asker's role clearly cannot reach an answer, and routing the request to the appropriate larger model. Screening for hostile input happens here too.

*Depth.* Authentication is Entra ID via OpenID Connect; the harness receives a token carrying group and role claims, and uses the on-behalf-of flow to exchange it for scoped tokens when calling downstream services. Entitlements are mapped from Entra groups to harness roles in one configuration store, versioned and reviewable.

The router model deserves a precise job description, because its title invites overclaiming. It classifies intent, performs a fast plausibility check of intent against entitlements, applies rate and scope limits, and selects the execution model — a small model for simple lookups, a frontier model for multi-step synthesis. This is a cost and experience optimization: most failures should fail in half a second with a courteous explanation, and most questions should not pay frontier-model prices. But the router is a triage nurse, not a judge. Language models can be persuaded; the router's decisions are therefore never the enforcement mechanism. Real authorization happens twice more, below, in systems that cannot be talked out of anything. Input screening uses Azure AI Content Safety's prompt-injection detection plus our own filters, applied before any request reaches an execution model.

### 5.3 The orchestration layer

This is the working floor: the selected model plans its steps, calls the tools the person's role permits, and assembles an answer with citations back to the systems the facts came from. Two design choices here matter more than the AI itself. First, the model chooses from a registry of narrow, typed tools rather than writing free-form queries — it can call "get advisee activity summary," not rummage through tables. Second, a semantic layer defines institutional terms exactly once, so "enrolled" means the same thing in every answer, and means what our federal reporting says it means.

*Depth.* The recommended agent runtime is Pydantic AI, and a distinction matters here: it is a library, not a service. What runs in production is an orchestration service we build — a stateless, containerized Python API that receives an authenticated request, runs the router step, executes the agent loop against the permitted tools, and returns a grounded answer — with Pydantic AI as a dependency inside it, the way any web application depends on its libraries. The reasoning for the choice: the whole security posture of this design rests on typed, validated tool contracts, and Pydantic is the de facto standard for typed validation in Python — the agent structure and the security philosophy are the same idea. The library has also matured to the point where it supplies most of what this service needs: the agent loop itself, native MCP client support, guardrail hooks, durable execution for long-running human-in-the-loop workflows (exactly the shape of the eventual propose-review-enact write pattern), and direct exposure of agents as FastAPI/Starlette web applications — the same stack the vendor documents as its production pattern, with same-vendor observability and evaluation tooling alongside. It remains model-agnostic — Anthropic, OpenAI, and Azure-hosted models interchange behind one interface. The code Franklin writes is therefore thin and entirely Franklin-specific: the identity exchange, the role-filtered tool registry, suppression, and audit — the parts no library could supply because they are made of our roles and our rules. The honest alternative is Microsoft's Semantic Kernel if the implementing team is a .NET shop; the choice should follow the builders' fluency, because the runtime is the most replaceable component in the system. What is not replaceable is the tool registry and its contracts, which should be defined as schemas independent of any runtime.

The semantic layer is dbt models over the lake, producing conformed marts whose measure definitions are documented against IPEDS definitions, plus a library of verified queries — parameterized, steward-reviewed SQL that the agent prefers over generated SQL wherever a question matches. Free-form text-to-SQL is permitted only against the semantic marts, never raw tables, and generated SQL is logged for review so the verified library grows from real usage. Answers must carry citations to their sources; an answer the system cannot ground, it declines to give.

### 5.4 The MCP tool layer

Each connected system gets its own small server speaking the Model Context Protocol — one for Canvas, one for Colleague, one for Kuali, one each for the external sources. Every server checks the user's identity against its own rules before returning anything. This is the layer that makes incremental rollout real: bringing a new system under the umbrella means building one new server against a written contract, not renovating the platform. It is also the layer that makes the harness durable — if we replace the agent runtime or the models in three years, these servers and their contracts remain.

*Depth.* Servers are built on the MCP Python SDK, deployed as containers in Azure (Container Apps is the sensible default), each validating the caller's Entra token as an OAuth resource server and enforcing tool-level and record-level authorization before touching its backing system. Servers expose read tools with explicit schemas and narrow semantics; no server exposes a generic "run this query" tool against an institutional system. Each server's authorization mapping (which roles may call which tools, with what record scoping) lives in its own configuration, reviewed by the data steward for that system — this is where the registrar's authority over Colleague data, for example, becomes enforceable code rather than a memo. Write tools, when they eventually exist, are separate tools with separate review, never a flag on a read tool.

### 5.5 The data layer

Most questions are answered not by poking live systems but by querying a governed copy of their data, refreshed on schedule, held in our own Azure environment. That copy — the lake — is where the strongest access enforcement lives: rules attached to the data itself decide, row by row and column by column, what each identity may see. The same rules apply whether the question arrives from the AI, from a dashboard, or from an analyst's SQL client. There is no privileged path.

*Depth.* The recommendation is Microsoft Fabric: lakehouse storage in OneLake, warehouse SQL endpoints for BI and analyst access, Entra-native security throughout, and Purview for classification, lineage, and sensitivity labeling. The reasoning is fit, not fashion — Franklin is an Entra/SQL Server institution, and Fabric means the security model, the identity model, and the operations skills are ones IT already holds. Databricks is the more powerful platform in the abstract; it is also a second security model and a second skills base for an institution that does not need either. Row-level security binds to Entra identity; column-level security and dynamic masking protect sensitive fields; small-cell suppression logic (our IPEDS suppression practice, generalized) is implemented in the semantic marts so that aggregate answers below threshold sizes are suppressed uniformly, no matter which path asked. Nightly extracts land raw, dbt builds the conformed layer, and freshness-sensitive questions (today's Canvas activity) go through the live MCP tools rather than the lake.

## 6. Integration points, system by system

**Colleague.** The system of record and the most sensitive source, so it comes under the umbrella last among the reads. Integration is through Ellucian's supported channels — Ethos APIs for live, narrow reads and scheduled extracts into the lake for analytics — never direct database coupling, which is fragile, unsupported, and a renewal-negotiation liability. The registrar is the data steward; the Colleague MCP server's authorization map is theirs to approve.

**Canvas.** Bulk analytics through Instructure's Canvas Data 2 (DAP) into the lake; live, current-day questions through the Canvas REST API behind the MCP server. The existing daily-digest proof of concept becomes the first consumer of this server — the same insights, now flowing through governed infrastructure, which converts a side pipeline into a sanctioned citizen of the platform.

**Kuali.** Curriculum and proposal data through Kuali's REST APIs, extracted on schedule. Low sensitivity, moderate value, and the key that makes curriculum-to-labor-market questions possible.

**IPEDS Oracle.** Already built and running as an institution-adjacent tool. Wrapping it as an MCP server is a contained, low-risk piece of work against public benchmarking data — which is exactly why it goes first. It proves the entire pattern with nothing sensitive at stake.

**BLS and O\*NET.** Public APIs for occupational projections, wages, and skills profiles. The join that makes these valuable is the NCES CIP-to-SOC crosswalk, which maps our academic programs to occupational codes; it should be loaded into the lake as a first-class reference table, because it is the hinge for every career-connectedness question.

**Job postings.** The recommendation is a licensed feed (Lightcast is the incumbent standard in higher education) rather than scraping. Scraping job boards is fragile, legally gray against most sites' terms, and produces data quality problems that poison downstream answers. This is the one integration with a real recurring data cost, and it is worth paying, because postings data is the freshest career-connectedness signal we can get.

## 7. Software and platform choices, stated plainly

For the executive reader: every choice below favors boring-where-possible. The novel part of this system is what it does, not what it is built from; the components are established, supported, and replaceable, and the two most important choices (identity and data platform) are the ones Franklin has effectively already made by being a Microsoft institution. In operational terms, Franklin runs very little: one orchestration API, a handful of small tool servers, and a gateway — stateless containers of a kind IT already operates — while data and models live on managed Azure platforms that are configured rather than run. The AI machinery itself is mature open source; what we build and own is the connective code that makes it Franklin's.

*Depth — the opinionated list.* Identity: Entra ID, OIDC, on-behalf-of token flow; no parallel identity store, ever. Data platform: Microsoft Fabric with dbt for transformation and Purview for governance. Models: Microsoft Foundry as the model plane — as of mid-2026 it offers both Anthropic's Claude models and OpenAI's models inside the Azure tenant, under Entra authentication and Azure billing, with a US data zone and a zero-retention option for sensitive workloads. That combination — frontier models from competing vendors, one governance surface, no data leaving our tenant's terms — is the cleanest answer available to "commercial APIs are acceptable with agreements." Router model: a small fast model (Haiku-class or 4o-mini-class); execution models: frontier-class, selected per task; all behind a thin abstraction so any model can be swapped without touching tools. Agent runtime: Pydantic AI, embedded as a library inside the orchestration service — one stateless container on Azure Container Apps, deployed alongside the MCP servers (Semantic Kernel acceptable if the builders are .NET-native). A note for vendor conversations: Pydantic also ships an add-on capabilities library coincidentally named "Pydantic AI Harness" — prebuilt components for code execution, guardrails, and sub-agent orchestration that the implementing team may adopt piecemeal. In this document, "the harness" always means Franklin's system, not that product. Tool layer: MCP Python SDK, containerized on Azure Container Apps. Input screening: Azure AI Content Safety plus custom filters. Observability: OpenTelemetry tracing of every agent step and tool call, with Pydantic Logfire or Azure Monitor as the sink — the trace and the audit log are separate records serving separate masters (engineering and compliance). Evaluations: a versioned suite of question-answer pairs with expected groundings, run in CI on every model, prompt, or schema change — Pydantic Evals is the natural companion to the recommended runtime, though promptfoo or a plain pytest structure serve equally well. Audit: append-only writes to immutable Azure storage, mirrored to Log Analytics for querying; the audit record includes user, role, question, tools called, records touched, and answer hash. FERPA posture: access under legitimate educational interest, demonstrated by the role-scoped enforcement and proven by the audit log; vendor data-processing agreements through the Azure/Foundry terms; zero-retention endpoints for any student-record traffic. One build-versus-buy question stays open by design: Azure's managed agent runtime inside Foundry could, in principle, replace the self-hosted orchestration container entirely, and the implementing team should formally evaluate it during phase one. The evaluation has a single acceptance test, and it is this document's non-negotiable: every tool call must execute under the individual user's identity, not the platform's service identity. Managed agent runtimes have historically failed exactly that test — tools run as the application, which is the confused-deputy design this architecture exists to prevent. If the managed service passes by the time we build, adopt it and operate one less container; if it does not, the self-hosted service has earned its keep, and we can say precisely why.

## 8. The security model, end to end

A skeptical reader should be able to trace one request and see where each protection sits, so here is the walk. A person signs in; Entra issues a short-lived token naming them and their roles. The router triages — helpfully, cheaply, and without authority. The execution model plans against only the tools that role can see; it holds no credentials and writes no free-form queries against institutional systems. Each tool call carries the user's token to an MCP server that independently re-validates it — the second enforcement point. The server's queries land on a lake whose row- and column-level rules check the same identity a third time. The answer returns through output safeguards: small-cell suppression, required citations, groundedness checks. Every step lands in the audit log.

The design assumes three uncomfortable truths. Language models can be manipulated, so no model's judgment is ever an enforcement mechanism — deceiving the router accomplishes nothing, because layers four and five do not listen to arguments. Data can be hostile — a Canvas discussion post is student-authored text that will eventually contain an instruction aimed at the AI reading it — so content-derived text is treated as data, never as instructions, and (in the read-write future) nothing derived from untrusted content flows into an action without human review. And aggregation leaks — "read-only" queries can re-identify students through small groups — so the suppression rules we already honor in federal reporting are enforced on every answer, uniformly, at the data layer where they cannot be bypassed.

Writes deserve their own sentences of restraint, and one distinction. Writes to course content — the kind CourseForge already performs when it pushes a course shell to Canvas — are low-sensitivity and reviewed by their nature, and they lead: this is where the write-tool pattern of typed tools, approval flow, and audit gets built and hardened, years before anything more sensitive depends on it. Writes to institutional records arrive last, one narrow tool at a time, each behind a propose-review-edit-enact workflow in which the human is the actor of record. In both cases the AI drafts; a person decides.

## 9. Building it: sequence, ownership, and the checklist

The build philosophy is spine first, organs one at a time. The spine — identity flow, gateway, audit, the empty tool registry, the lake's security scaffolding — must exist in version one even though version one connects to a single, harmless system, because these are the components no project successfully retrofits. Every failed enterprise AI effort I can point to deferred authorization until after "proving value," then proved value on an architecture that could not support it. The scope of the build is also smaller than the word "platform" suggests: the agent loop, MCP plumbing, durable human-in-the-loop execution, and evaluation tooling now ship in mature open-source libraries, so the paid work — internal or contracted — is integration and governance code: identity exchange, the tool registry and its role filters, suppression, and audit. A proposal that prices the construction of an orchestration engine is pricing something that already exists, and should be read skeptically.

The rollout order follows sensitivity, ascending: first the IPEDS Oracle (already built, public data, zero FERPA exposure — it proves the whole pipeline with nothing at stake); second BLS/O\*NET and job postings (still external, and they buy a second constituency in career connectedness before any student record is touched); third Canvas (first internal system, with two running starts — the existing daily-digest work becomes the Canvas server's first read consumer, and CourseForge's course push becomes its first write consumer, on low-stakes content); fourth Colleague through the lake, read-only, after the security machinery has been exercised on lower-stakes data and the registrar has co-authored the definitions; Kuali wherever demand appears; record writes after everything, one tool at a time, with content writes arriving earlier through CourseForge as section 8 describes. Alongside phase one, CourseForge points its model calls at the institutional model plane — no functional change to the tool, immediate inheritance of institutional governance, and the first demonstration that the harness serves applications, not just chat.

On ownership: the vision does not require the visionary to build it, but it does require a named internal technical owner — a platform engineer in IT, or a designated lead if a consulting firm builds the spine. The workable division is this: a consulting engagement can build the spine and the first two MCP servers against this document, with knowledge transfer as an explicit deliverable; internal IT owns operations, the onboarding checklist, and every subsequent server. What cannot be outsourced is stewardship — the definitions in the semantic layer and the authorization maps belong to Franklin employees (registrar, IR, IT security), because consultants leave and definitions stay.

Each new source comes under the umbrella through the same checklist, and the checklist is the governance story:

1. A named data steward has approved the server's authorization map — which roles reach which tools, scoped to which records.
2. The semantic definitions the source contributes are documented and, where applicable, reconciled to IPEDS definitions.
3. An evaluation set for the source's questions exists and passes before launch.
4. Suppression and sensitivity rules are reviewed for the new data.
5. Audit fields are confirmed flowing for every tool.

By the third system this checklist is a routine; by the fifth it is the reason a new source takes weeks rather than quarters.

## 10. Risks and honest objections

**"Why not wait for Ellucian?"** Ellucian's 2026 platform ships agentic AI inside its own walls, and for workflows that live entirely within Ellucian systems it may serve well. But its agents will never join our Canvas signals to our curriculum data to BLS projections to our IPEDS benchmarking, because no vendor federates sources it does not own. The harness treats Ellucian as one respected source among six. And no vendor platform will ever sit beneath an internally built tool the way the harness sits beneath CourseForge — the ability to power our own applications, not just answer questions, is exclusive to infrastructure we own. The honest framing: we are applying access-control architecture that regulated industries consider table stakes to a problem vendors would prefer to solve for us on their terms. Roughly four-fifths of this design is proven enterprise practice; most of the rest is current best practice that few institutions have assembled; the genuinely novel sliver is carrying per-user identity through agentic tool calls — which is precisely the part worth owning.

**Wrong answers.** The mitigations are structural: verified queries preferred over generated ones, a single semantic layer, mandatory citations, refusal when grounding fails, and the evaluation suite that catches quiet regressions when a model or schema changes. The system will still sometimes be wrong; the design goal is that it is wrong visibly, traceably, and correctably.

**Prompt injection and hostile content.** Addressed structurally in section 8; the summary is that no model's compliance is ever the thing standing between a user and data they should not see.

**Perception risk.** Faculty may hear "AI over Canvas data" as surveillance. The answer is candor and scope: the system answers questions people are already entitled to ask, faster, with better logging than the status quo — and the non-goals in section 3 should be communicated as prominently as the vision.

**Cost and drift.** The counterweights are the incremental sequence (each phase is separately fundable and separately stoppable), the checklist (which caps per-source cost), and the replaceable-runtime posture (which caps vendor exposure).

## 11. Cost shape

Order-of-magnitude, for planning posture rather than budgeting: the spine plus the first two servers is a bounded build — a focused consulting engagement measured in a few hundred thousand dollars, or two to three internal quarters if IT can dedicate a platform engineer and fractional data engineering. The bound holds because the orchestration machinery is mature open source (section 9); the money buys integration and governance work, not engine construction. Run costs are modest: model usage at institutional scale bills in the low thousands per month through Azure, Fabric capacity is likely an extension of commitments Franklin is already making, and the one genuine new recurring line is the licensed postings feed. The dominant ongoing cost is a fraction of a person: someone who owns the platform, runs the checklist, and tends the evaluation suite. A system like this is cheap to run and expensive only to neglect.

The return. The honest way to quantify return on infrastructure is break-even arithmetic, not projected percentages, so use conservative assumptions: a typical student completes about 60 credit hours with us at $398 per credit hour — roughly $24,000 in tuition revenue over their time at Franklin. Against a build measured in a few hundred thousand dollars, the entire spine-plus-first-servers investment is repaid by thirteen to seventeen additional retained students. Not per year — in total, over the system's life. Even discounting tuition heavily for instructional cost, break-even remains under three dozen students: a fraction of a percent of enrollment. The retention interventions in section 2 — an advisor learning which advisees have gone quiet this week, while there is still time to act — do not happen today because they are too expensive in staff time; this system makes them nearly free. No forecast is required. The only question is whether to bet against a number that small.

The second return is capacity rather than cash: analysts and advisors currently spend real hours retrieving and reconciling data across systems before their actual work can begin. Governed self-service returns those hours to the work that requires their judgment — analysis, intervention, advising — and collapses turnaround from days to seconds.

The third is avoidance: vendor AI arrives as recurring platform uplifts, and ungoverned AI use of institutional data is already a sector-wide reality; owned, governed infrastructure substitutes for some of the former and insures against the latter.

The fourth return compounds. Today, every new AI use case at Franklin pays full price: its own model integration, its own credentials, its own security review, its own FERPA analysis, its own audit answer — or it quietly skips some of these, which is a different kind of expensive. The harness converts that from a recurring project into a checklist (section 9): the first system under the umbrella pays the fixed cost of the spine, and every system after pays only the marginal cost of one tool server and one steward review — quarters of effort becoming weeks. This is the return that grows with demand: AI use cases will keep arriving whether or not we build shared infrastructure; the harness only changes what each arrival costs, and what risks it carries in with it.

All of this is entitled to skepticism, which is why the system measures its own case: the audit log doubles as a usage record, and each phase is proposed against the observed use of the one before. No phase asks for faith in a forecast; each asks for a decision about evidence.

## 12. Decisions requested

This document asks for four things, because a vision with no decision attached gets admired and shelved. First, endorsement of the architecture's one non-negotiable — per-user identity end to end, enforced at three layers — as the standard any build, internal or contracted, must meet. Second, designation of data stewards for Colleague, Canvas, and Kuali data (registrar, IR, and academic affairs respectively is the natural map). Third, a named internal technical owner, with a decision on the internal-versus-contracted split for the spine. Fourth, authorization to proceed with phase one — the spine plus the IPEDS Oracle and labor-market servers — as a separately funded, separately evaluable step.

- **Harness** — the institution-owned AI layer that connects people to systems; the umbrella, not any single tool.
- **CourseForge** — Franklin's internally built AI course-authoring tool, owned by instructional design; the first existing application to connect to the harness as a surface.
- **MCP (Model Context Protocol)** — an open standard for connecting AI systems to tools and data; each of our sources becomes one MCP server.
- **PEP (policy enforcement point)** — a place where authorization is independently checked; this design has three.
- **Token** — a short-lived, signed statement of who a user is and what roles they hold, issued at sign-in and carried through every layer.
- **Semantic layer** — the single place where institutional terms are defined as data logic, so "enrolled" means one thing everywhere.
- **Row-level security** — rules attached to data itself that filter what each identity can see, regardless of the application asking.
- **Small-cell suppression** — withholding aggregate results about very small groups so individuals cannot be identified; standard practice in federal reporting, applied here to every answer.
- **Entra ID** — Microsoft's identity platform, Franklin's existing sign-in.
- **Fabric / OneLake** — Microsoft's data platform, where the governed lake lives.
- **Foundry** — Microsoft's service for running commercial AI models (including Anthropic's and OpenAI's) inside an Azure environment under institutional governance.
- **Guardrails** — automated screening applied to requests coming in (hostile input) and answers going out (suppression, citations).
- **Audit log** — the append-only record of who asked what, when, under which role, touching which records.
