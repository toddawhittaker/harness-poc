## 1. The problem and the thesis

Franklin runs on capable systems that do not talk to each other, and on people who spend their scarce time being the integration layer. An advisor who wants to know which advisees have gone quiet must check Canvas, then Colleague, then their own notes. A chair comparing a program's outcomes against the labor market must pull IPEDS data, BLS projections, and enrollment reports from three places and reconcile them by hand. Institutional Research answers the same questions repeatedly because the answers live in systems most people cannot query. The information exists; the connective tissue does not.

The thesis of this project is that the connective tissue should be a single, institution-owned AI layer — a harness — that sits above our systems, answers questions in plain language, and enforces our access rules at every step. One harness, many doors: a chat surface, an advisor view, a reporting surface, and programmatic access for analysts, all drawing on the same governed connections to Canvas, Colleague, Kuali, our IPEDS benchmarking tool, federal labor data, and job-market signals. People ask questions in the role they hold and receive answers scoped to that role, with every question and answer logged.

The architecture described here has one organizing principle: **the user's own identity travels with every request, end to end, and authorization is enforced independently, at more than one layer, by the systems that own the data.** The AI never acts as an all-seeing service account. Whatever the model does, it does as the specific advisor, chair, or dean who asked — and no more. This principle is what makes the rest of the design trustworthy, and it is the part that cannot be retrofitted later.

## 2. What this gets us

The value of this system is not "AI capability." It is specific questions that people at Franklin currently cannot answer, or can answer only through days of manual work, answered in seconds through infrastructure we govern. One example per constituency:

**An advisor** asks, "Which of my advisees haven't logged into Canvas in the past ten days, and how are they doing in their current courses?" Today this requires checking two systems student by student, so in practice it doesn't happen until a problem surfaces on its own. With the harness, it is one question, answered only for the students that advisor is assigned — the access rules see to that.

**A program chair** asks, "How did midterm performance in our capstone compare across the last four terms, and did the course revision change anything?" Today this is a request to IR with a queue behind it. With the harness, it is a self-service question against governed data, using the same definitions IR would use.

**A dean** asks, "Which of our programs feed the ten fastest-growing occupations in central Ohio, and where are our completion numbers relative to peer institutions?" Today, no one asks this, because answering it means joining our program data to BLS projections and IPEDS benchmarks by hand. With the harness, it is a single question spanning three sources — the kind of synthesis no vendor product will do for us, because no vendor federates our career-connectedness data with our institutional data.

**Career services** asks, "What skills appear most often in job postings for the roles our MSIS graduates take, and where do our course outcomes not cover them?" This connects postings data to curriculum data in Kuali — again, a join that exists nowhere today.

**An instructional designer** working in CourseForge, our internally built AI course-authoring tool, asks, "Which skills appear in postings for this program's target roles that our current course outcomes don't cover — and draft activities that would address them." Today CourseForge designs from the designer's knowledge and the AI's general training. Connected to the harness, it designs from those plus the institution's evidence: labor-market data, curriculum outcomes in Kuali, and what has actually engaged students in comparable courses.

**An analyst** connects directly to the data lake with SQL and sees exactly what their role permits, because the access rules live in the data platform itself, not in any one application. Power users get power tools without a side door around governance.

The pattern across all six: the harness does not create new data. It makes existing data answerable, by the right people, within the rules, with a record of who asked.

## 3. What this is not

Scope anxiety kills projects like this more often than technical failure does, so the boundaries deserve to be stated as plainly as the vision.

This is not a replacement for Colleague, Canvas, or any system of record. Those systems keep doing what they do; the harness reads from them and, much later and narrowly, may write to them through supervised channels. This is not a public-facing chatbot; every user is an authenticated Franklin employee with a defined role. This is not a surveillance tool; the same questions faculty and staff can ask today through slower means become faster, and nothing beyond that becomes visible — the access rules that govern people govern the AI acting on their behalf. Nothing in this system takes automatic action on a student record; in the eventual read-write phase, the AI proposes, a human reviews and edits, and only the human enacts. And this is not a bet on any single AI vendor; the model layer is deliberately interchangeable, and the durable assets — the data definitions, the tool contracts, the access model, the audit trail — are ours regardless of whose model answers on a given day.

## 4. The architecture in one pass

The system has five layers, drawn top to bottom in the companion diagram, with a set of services that apply everywhere. A request enters at the top and returns up the same path.

**Surfaces** are the ways in: a chat interface, an advisor workspace, a reporting view, programmatic API access, direct SQL for analysts, and existing applications — beginning with CourseForge, our AI course-authoring tool — connecting as clients. Surfaces we build new are deliberately thin, and none of them contains business logic or access decisions; existing applications keep their own interfaces and draw on the harness for what sits beneath.

**The front door** authenticates the person through our existing Microsoft sign-in, attaches their roles, and uses a small, inexpensive AI model to triage the request: classify what is being asked, decline what is out of scope, and select which larger model should do the real work. Input screening for hostile or malformed requests happens here.

**The orchestration layer** is where the selected model works: planning steps, choosing among the tools the person's role permits, and drawing on a semantic layer where terms like "enrolled student" and "retention" are defined exactly once, aligned with our IPEDS reporting definitions.

**The tool layer** exposes each connected system — Canvas, Colleague, Kuali, the IPEDS Oracle, BLS/O*NET, job postings — as an independent server speaking the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard for connecting AI systems to tools. Each server re-validates the user's identity against its own access rules. The harness is a client of these servers, never a superuser over them.

**The data layer** is a governed lake in our Azure environment, fed by scheduled extracts from institutional systems, with row- and column-level security bound to the same identity that entered at the front door. Analysts who connect directly hit the same rules as the AI.

Two of these layers — the tool servers and the data platform — independently enforce authorization. These are labeled "PEP" (policy enforcement point) in the diagram. If either is misconfigured or deceived, the other still holds. The user's identity token, shown as the continuous thread down the left of the diagram, is what both check. The front door is drawn differently on purpose: it triages, it declines what is obviously out of scope, and it holds no authority, because a language model's judgment is never allowed to be the thing standing between a person and a record. Counting it as a third enforcement point would flatter the design, and it would mislead the first security reviewer who read the code.

The orchestration layer sits between them and is a third role-based control, deliberately not counted as a third enforcement point. It does validate the user's token independently — every service in this design does, and none takes the layer above it on trust — and it does make a role-based decision, filtering the tool registry so that a tool the caller's roles cannot reach is never placed in the model's view. That is worth having: it keeps the model from being tempted by, and then refused on, tools it was never going to be allowed to call. But the test of an enforcement point is what happens when it fails *open*, and this one has no consequence when it does. A stale registry, a filtering bug, or a caller who skips the orchestrator entirely changes nothing, because the tool server refuses on its own. A layer whose permissive failure costs nothing is defense in depth rather than a boundary, and the distinction is worth keeping crisp, because it is the reason the design can afford to let the router fail open too.

Independent enforcement is not the same as independent premises, and the distinction decides where the real risk sits. Both enforcement points evaluate the same input — the roles asserted in the user's token — and both derive it from one place: the mapping from Entra groups to harness roles. A person in the wrong group is granted access by every layer, confidently and in writing. Checking a wrong premise twice is not defense in depth. The entitlement mapping is therefore the highest-criticality component in this system, and §5.2 treats it as one.

Alongside all of this run the cross-cutting services. The identity provider is the single source of truth for roles. An **authorization rulebook** states every access rule exactly once, and each enforcing layer evaluates a cached copy of it rather than rules somebody typed in separately. A **relationship directory** holds the facts those rules turn on — which advisor is assigned to which student, which instructor teaches which section — refreshed from the systems of record on a published schedule. A **person crosswalk** records that one human being is the same human being in Colleague, Canvas, Kuali, and Entra, without which no cross-source answer can be trusted. A **delegation register** governs work that must act for someone who is not signed in. An append-only **audit log** records who asked what, when, and under which role. **Output safeguards** suppress small-cell results, require answers to cite their sources, and mechanically verify that the sources cited were actually reached. An **evaluation suite** re-tests the system against known question-answer pairs whenever a model or schema changes. A **switchboard** can disable a tool, a source, a role, a surface, or one person immediately and without a deployment. And, in the later read-write phase, a human approval workflow governs any action that changes a record.

Five of those — the rulebook, the relationship directory, the person crosswalk, the delegation register, and the switchboard — are named here because the original draft of this document assumed them rather than specifying them, and each is markedly cheaper to build with the spine than to retrofit across six servers afterwards.

## 5. The layers in detail

### 5.1 Surfaces

The surfaces are thin by design: they render, they collect input, and they hold nothing that matters. This is what makes "one harness, many doors" honest. Because every access decision lives below the surface layer, adding a new surface — or letting a power user point a standards-compliant client at our tool servers — adds no new risk. Any client, ours or not, gets identical enforcement.

*Depth.* The chat surface ships first because it exercises the entire pipeline at the lowest build cost. The advisor workspace and reporting views come later, composed over the same tool layer, and only once enough tools exist beneath them to justify the build. The reporting surface should lean on Power BI against the lake's semantic models rather than reinventing visualization; the harness's contribution there is natural-language authoring and cross-source questions, not chart rendering. Analyst SQL access goes through the lake's SQL endpoints with the same Entra identity — no separate credentials, no separate rules.

CourseForge is the first existing application to take a place among the surfaces, and it matters to the strategy out of proportion to its size. Built internally by our instructional design group, it already supports bi-directional, ID-led course design with AI personas and pushes initial courses to Canvas — which makes it proof of institutional appetite and a day-one customer for the platform. It remains its own product with its own owners and roadmap; what changes is what sits beneath it, in stages. Its model calls move to the institutional model plane, inheriting governance, billing, and evaluation discipline with no functional change. Its Canvas push moves behind the Canvas server's write tools, giving the institution its first hardened write path on deliberately low-stakes content. And as sources come online, its design personas gain access to labor-market, curriculum, and course-performance data that no standalone, prompt-engineered tool could reach. CourseForge gains an institution behind it; the harness gains a customer that already exists.

### 5.2 The front door: identity, triage, screening

Everyone enters through the sign-in Franklin already uses. That sign-in produces a short-lived token naming the person and their roles, and that token — not a shared system account — is what every downstream layer sees. A small, fast AI model then performs triage: understanding what kind of question this is, declining early and politely when the asker's role clearly cannot reach an answer, and routing the request to the appropriate larger model. Screening for hostile input happens here too.

*Depth.* Authentication is Entra ID via OpenID Connect; the harness receives a token carrying group and role claims, and uses the on-behalf-of flow to exchange it for scoped tokens when calling downstream services. Entitlements are mapped from Entra groups to harness roles in one configuration store, versioned and reviewable.

That store deserves more than a clause, because it is the premise every enforcement point below depends on (§4). It is changed through review rather than through a portal; its changes raise their own alerts; the joiner-mover-leaver process is wired to it, so that a role disappears when a job does; and it goes through periodic access review with a named owner. Two practical notes for whoever builds it. Entra stops emitting group claims once a user belongs to more than roughly two hundred groups and substitutes a lookup reference instead, which downstream code will read as *no roles at all* — so harness roles should ride on app roles or a claims-mapping policy rather than on raw group membership. And the audience claim can arrive as a list where naive validation expects a single string; the proof of concept in this repository already carries a note to that effect against the day the issuer stops being a stub.

Roles carry a scope, not only a title. "Dean" is a job; "dean of the College of Business" is a job and a boundary, and nearly every role in a university has one — this college, this department, this campus, this term. The scope travels with the role, the rulebook names which tools are scope-sensitive, and the tool server filters on it. Advising is the deliberate exception, because it is governed by assignment rather than by organizational boundary, which is what the record-level checks in §5.4 are for. Getting this right before the second dean exists is far cheaper than widening an unscoped role afterwards and re-examining every answer ever given under it.

The switchboard is read here too, on every request, because it must be cheap and it must apply to everything above and below. It can disable a tool, a source, a role, a surface, or one person, immediately and without a deployment, at every boundary the rulebook already understands. Nothing in this design is more predictable than being asked for it in the first meeting after an incident, and nothing is more commonly built last.

The router model deserves a precise job description, because its title invites overclaiming. It classifies intent, performs a fast plausibility check of intent against entitlements, applies rate and scope limits, and selects the execution model — a small model for simple lookups, a frontier model for multi-step synthesis. This is a cost and experience optimization: most failures should fail in half a second with a courteous explanation, and most questions should not pay frontier-model prices. But the router is a triage nurse, not a judge. Language models can be persuaded; the router's decisions are therefore never the enforcement mechanism. Real authorization happens twice more, below, in systems that cannot be talked out of anything. Input screening uses Azure AI Content Safety's prompt-injection detection plus our own filters, applied before any request reaches an execution model.

### 5.3 The orchestration layer

This is the working floor: the selected model plans its steps, calls the tools the person's role permits, and assembles an answer with citations back to the systems the facts came from. Two design choices here matter more than the AI itself. First, the model chooses from a registry of narrow, typed tools rather than writing free-form queries — it can call "get advisee activity summary," not rummage through tables. Second, a semantic layer defines institutional terms exactly once, so "enrolled" means the same thing in every answer, and means what our federal reporting says it means.

*Depth.* The recommended agent runtime is Pydantic AI, and a distinction matters here: it is a library, not a service. What runs in production is an orchestration service we build — a stateless, containerized Python API that receives an authenticated request, runs the router step, executes the agent loop against the permitted tools, and returns a grounded answer — with Pydantic AI as a dependency inside it, the way any web application depends on its libraries. The reasoning for the choice: the whole security posture of this design rests on typed, validated tool contracts, and Pydantic is the de facto standard for typed validation in Python — the agent structure and the security philosophy are the same idea. The library has also matured to the point where it supplies most of what this service needs: the agent loop itself, native MCP client support, guardrail hooks, durable execution for long-running human-in-the-loop workflows (exactly the shape of the eventual propose-review-enact write pattern), and direct exposure of agents as FastAPI/Starlette web applications — the same stack the vendor documents as its production pattern, with same-vendor observability and evaluation tooling alongside. It remains model-agnostic — Anthropic, OpenAI, and Azure-hosted models interchange behind one interface. The code Franklin writes is therefore thin and entirely Franklin-specific: the identity exchange, the role-filtered tool registry, suppression, and audit — the parts no library could supply because they are made of our roles and our rules. The honest alternative is Microsoft's Semantic Kernel if the implementing team is a .NET shop; the choice should follow the builders' fluency, because the runtime is the most replaceable component in the system. What is not replaceable is the tool registry and its contracts, which should be defined as schemas independent of any runtime.

The semantic layer is dbt models over the lake, producing conformed marts whose measure definitions are documented against IPEDS definitions, plus a library of verified queries — parameterized, steward-reviewed SQL that the agent prefers over generated SQL wherever a question matches. Free-form text-to-SQL is permitted only against the semantic marts, never raw tables, and generated SQL is logged for review so the verified library grows from real usage. Answers must carry citations to their sources; an answer the system cannot ground, it declines to give.

Grounding is checked mechanically, not requested politely. Telling a model to answer only from tool results is an instruction to a model, and this design's whole argument is that a model's compliance is never the thing relied upon. So before an answer is returned, the citations it carries are compared against the servers a tool call actually reached on that request; an answer citing a source that never ran is regenerated once, and if it fails again the system declines rather than guesses. The proof of concept measured why this is not optional. In three runs out of ten, a model asked about a section it was not allowed to see invented twenty-four students and cited two systems it had never called — and nothing in the audit trail contradicted it, because nothing in the audit trail existed. That is the general lesson of this architecture applied to the return path: making a bad outcome impossible holds, and asking a model not to produce one decays.

Work that runs when nobody is signed in needs an explicit answer, and it is the likeliest place for this document's one non-negotiable to be excepted quietly rather than repealed openly. The on-behalf-of flow requires a user to be present. Scheduled per-person analysis runs at three in the morning; a propose-review-enact workflow pauses for days and resumes. Neither can carry a live user token, and the path of least resistance — a service principal asserting a user context — is precisely the all-seeing account this design exists to prevent. The answer is the delegation register: the person consents once, the grant is narrow, time-boxed, and revocable, every scheduled run records which grant it acted under, and grants appear in access review and expire without anyone remembering to remove them. Work that merely pauses is handled more simply, by re-authenticating the human at the moment of the act — better governance than holding credentials for three days, and less to build. This decision belongs in phase one even though the first unattended workload comes later, because a decision made under deadline is how the exception gets made.

One service does not mean one pool. The orchestration service is stateless, which means it scales by running more copies; it does not mean unlike work can safely share them. Interactive questions with a person waiting, nightly per-person batches, long-form generation for CourseForge, and programmatic access that may be automated have nothing in common but the code path, and sharing one pool between them means the first badly tuned batch job takes down advising during the first week of registration — and whoever wrote the batch job will not know they did it. Same code, same rulebook, separate deployments and quotas per class of work. The same isolation applies downstream: a timeout per connected system, a circuit breaker that stops calling one that is already failing, and a cap on how much of the pool any single slow source may occupy. Without those, one sluggish Ethos endpoint becomes a slow harness for everybody, including people asking questions that never touch Colleague.

### 5.4 The MCP tool layer

Each connected system gets its own small server speaking the Model Context Protocol — one for Canvas, one for Colleague, one for Kuali, one each for the external sources. Every server checks the user's identity against its own rules before returning anything. This is the layer that makes incremental rollout real: bringing a new system under the umbrella means building one new server against a written contract, not renovating the platform. It is also the layer that makes the harness durable — if we replace the agent runtime or the models in three years, these servers and their contracts remain.

*Depth.* Servers are built on the MCP Python SDK, deployed as containers in Azure (Container Apps is the sensible default), each validating the caller's Entra token as an OAuth resource server and enforcing tool-level and record-level authorization before touching its backing system. Servers expose read tools with explicit schemas and narrow semantics; no server exposes a generic "run this query" tool against an institutional system. Each server's authorization mapping (which roles may call which tools, with what record scoping) lives in its own configuration, reviewed by the data steward for that system — this is where the registrar's authority over Colleague data, for example, becomes enforceable code rather than a memo. Write tools, when they eventually exist, are separate tools with separate review, never a flag on a read tool.

Two things keep this layer from becoming six divergent policies as sources accumulate. The first is that enforcement is distributed and authorship is not. Each server still decides locally, in its own process, on the caller's own token, with no live call to anything — that property is what makes the layer trustworthy and it does not change. What changes is where the rules come from. One versioned rulebook states, for every tool, which roles may call it, what scope limits apply, which relationship must exist for record-level access, and what denies access outright; each server evaluates a cached copy; and continuous integration proves that each server enforces exactly what the rulebook says. The steward still approves, and approves a change to one reviewable document rather than a pull request against a service they cannot read. Without this, by the sixth source the institution's access policy exists as fragments across six repositories plus the data platform plus Purview, and nobody can answer the question that begins every audit, every records request, and every annual access review: what can a dean see?

The second is the relationship directory, because the interesting checks in this design are not about job titles at all. "Advisor" is a role and lives in Entra; "advisor of student S-1055" is a relationship and lives in Colleague; "instructor of section CYB-310-A" is a relationship and lives in Canvas. A server needing a relationship it does not own must otherwise either call the most sensitive and most rate-limited system in the estate on its authorization path, or keep its own copy and let the two drift. So the relationships that scope access are published into one small, fast directory, refreshed from the systems of record on a stated schedule, with the staleness window published rather than implied — "advising assignments as of 04:00 today" is a fact somebody must be able to state under oath. It holds facts, not policy; the rulebook decides what those facts permit; and when it disagrees with a system of record, the system of record wins and the directory is wrong. Alongside the relationships sit individual grants — an interim chair covering a program for a term, an accreditation reviewer with six weeks of read access — each recorded with a scope, a reason, an expiry, and a grantor, so that one person's extra access never has to become an invented role that nobody ever deletes. And the model needs a denial that is evaluated last and beats everything else, because roles add up and recusal does not: a dean excluded from their own department's data during a grievance is a rule that a union of permissions cannot express.

Every tool contract carries four things beyond its arguments and returns, and they are part of the contract rather than runtime behavior. Its authorization rule, as a reference into the rulebook. An "as of" timestamp, so that freshness is a property of the answer rather than a side effect of which tool the model happened to choose — otherwise two people asking the same question in different words receive numbers from different days, both correctly cited and inconsistent with each other. A maximum row count. And what the server does instead when that count is exceeded, which is to aggregate rather than to truncate silently. Size, not authorization, is where systems like this actually fall over: a question reaching for every student in a college produces a result that will not fit in a context window, costs real money to attempt, and yields a worse answer than a refusal would have. Declaring the limit turns that into a condition the answer can state — "this covers 200 of 1,340 students; ask for a program rollup instead."

Finally, the contract is the durable asset and the protocol is not. MCP is young and has already changed both its transport and its authorization model. Tools are therefore defined once — arguments, returns, authorization, freshness, limits — in a repository with no protocol dependency, and the MCP binding is generated from that definition. A protocol change then becomes a regeneration rather than a project, a second protocol becomes a second binding, and the same definitions can serve a plain REST interface for analysts without anyone implementing the rules twice.

### 5.5 The data layer

Most questions are answered not by poking live systems but by querying a governed copy of their data, refreshed on schedule, held in our own Azure environment. That copy — the lake — is where the strongest access enforcement lives: rules attached to the data itself decide, row by row and column by column, what each identity may see. The same rules apply whether the question arrives from the AI, from a dashboard, or from an analyst's SQL client. There is no privileged path.

*Depth.* The recommendation is Microsoft Fabric: lakehouse storage in OneLake, warehouse SQL endpoints for BI and analyst access, Entra-native security throughout, and Purview for classification, lineage, and sensitivity labeling. The reasoning is fit, not fashion — Franklin is an Entra/SQL Server institution, and Fabric means the security model, the identity model, and the operations skills are ones IT already holds. Databricks is the more powerful platform in the abstract; it is also a second security model and a second skills base for an institution that does not need either. Row-level security binds to Entra identity; column-level security and dynamic masking protect sensitive fields; small-cell suppression logic (our IPEDS suppression practice, generalized) is implemented in the semantic marts so that aggregate answers below threshold sizes are suppressed uniformly, no matter which path asked. Nightly extracts land raw, dbt builds the conformed layer, and freshness-sensitive questions (today's Canvas activity) go through the live MCP tools rather than the lake.

Two caveats belong in the same section as the claim, because both are ways the strongest enforcement point turns out to have a side door. The first is that row- and column-level rules defined on a warehouse govern queries arriving through the warehouse; direct access to the underlying files — a Spark notebook, a shortcut, a workspace role granted for a good reason — is governed by a different mechanism, and if the two are not deliberately aligned then the layer described as the one nothing can route around has a route around it. This has a definite answer in configuration, and the answer needs to be in writing before Colleague data lands. The second is that suppression implemented in the semantic marts covers the aggregates those marts define, and does not cover an analyst composing two overlapping queries whose difference identifies one student. That is a query-time disclosure-control problem rather than a modeling one. Uniform suppression at the data layer remains the right ambition; prepared marts alone do not deliver it, so state what is covered and commission a disclosure-control review for the rest.

The lake is also where the person crosswalk lives, and it is a prerequisite rather than a convenience. Every cross-source answer in §2 depends on one human being recognizable as the same human being in Colleague, Canvas, Kuali, and Entra — and that mapping is a maintained asset with a match rate below one hundred percent, not a table somebody already has. Every failure is either a row silently missing from an answer or a row silently wrong, and the first is the more dangerous, because an advisor cannot see the student who was dropped from the list. It needs a named owner, a measured and reported match rate, and answers that say what they could not resolve: "this covers 22 of your 25 advisees; three could not be matched to Canvas activity this week" is worth far more than a list of twenty-two presented as a list of all.

## 6. Integration points, system by system

**Colleague.** The system of record and the most sensitive source, so it comes under the umbrella last among the reads. Integration is through Ellucian's supported channels — Ethos APIs for live, narrow reads and scheduled extracts into the lake for analytics — never direct database coupling, which is fragile, unsupported, and a renewal-negotiation liability. The registrar is the data steward; the Colleague MCP server's authorization map is theirs to approve. Plan the fallback explicitly rather than discovering it under pressure: Ethos has coverage gaps, its data model is not Colleague's data model and the translation is lossy in places that matter for reporting, and when a needed read turns out not to be available, the pull toward something less supported will arrive at the worst possible moment. The decision to make in advance is that the fallback is a scheduled extract into the lake, never a direct database read.

**Canvas.** Bulk analytics through Instructure's Canvas Data 2 (DAP) into the lake; live, current-day questions through the Canvas REST API behind the MCP server. The existing daily-digest proof of concept becomes the first consumer of this server — the same insights, now flowing through governed infrastructure, which converts a side pipeline into a sanctioned citizen of the platform.

**Kuali.** Curriculum and proposal data through Kuali's REST APIs, extracted on schedule. Low sensitivity, moderate value, and the key that makes curriculum-to-labor-market questions possible.

**IPEDS Oracle.** Already built and running as an institution-adjacent tool. Wrapping it as an MCP server is a contained, low-risk piece of work against public benchmarking data — which is exactly why it goes first. It proves the entire pattern with nothing sensitive at stake.

**BLS and O\*NET.** Public APIs for occupational projections, wages, and skills profiles. The join that makes these valuable is the NCES CIP-to-SOC crosswalk, which maps our academic programs to occupational codes; it should be loaded into the lake as a first-class reference table, because it is the hinge for every career-connectedness question.

**Job postings.** The recommendation is a licensed feed (Lightcast is the incumbent standard in higher education) rather than scraping. Scraping job boards is fragile, legally gray against most sites' terms, and produces data quality problems that poison downstream answers. This is the one integration with a real recurring data cost, and it is worth paying, because postings data is the freshest career-connectedness signal we can get. One clause to read before the architecture depends on it: licenses in this category commonly restrict redistribution, retention of derived data, and exposure through interfaces reachable by people who are not named users. "Loaded into our lake, where an AI answers questions from it for anyone holding a role" is exactly the pattern those clauses are written about. Negotiate it if it is wrong, because the join it enables is the most distinctive thing this system does.

**The join key itself.** Not a system, and the integration nobody budgets for. Every answer in §2 assumes a person, a course, and a program can be recognized across Colleague, Canvas, Kuali, and Entra. Building and maintaining that crosswalk — with an owner, a measured match rate, and unresolved records surfaced rather than dropped — is a first-class piece of work and a prerequisite for the cross-source questions, not a detail inside any one integration. The same holds for the CIP-to-SOC crosswalk above: a small table encoding a large amount of judgment about which programs feed which occupations, many-to-many, and productive of plausible nonsense when joined naively. It belongs to academic affairs, not to a load script.

## 7. Software and platform choices, stated plainly

For the executive reader: every choice below favors boring-where-possible. The novel part of this system is what it does, not what it is built from; the components are established, supported, and replaceable, and the two most important choices (identity and data platform) are the ones Franklin has effectively already made by being a Microsoft institution. In operational terms, Franklin runs very little: one orchestration API, a handful of small tool servers, and a gateway — stateless containers of a kind IT already operates — while data and models live on managed Azure platforms that are configured rather than run. The AI machinery itself is mature open source; what we build and own is the connective code that makes it Franklin's.

*Depth — the opinionated list.* Identity: Entra ID, OIDC, on-behalf-of token flow; no parallel identity store, ever. Data platform: Microsoft Fabric with dbt for transformation and Purview for governance. Models: Microsoft Foundry as the model plane — as of mid-2026 it offers both Anthropic's Claude models and OpenAI's models inside the Azure tenant, under Entra authentication and Azure billing, with a US data zone and a zero-retention option for sensitive workloads. That combination — frontier models from competing vendors, one governance surface, no data leaving our tenant's terms — is the cleanest answer available to "commercial APIs are acceptable with agreements." Router model: a small fast model (Haiku-class or 4o-mini-class); execution models: frontier-class, selected per task; all behind a thin abstraction so any model can be swapped without touching tools. Agent runtime: Pydantic AI, embedded as a library inside the orchestration service — one stateless container on Azure Container Apps, deployed alongside the MCP servers (Semantic Kernel acceptable if the builders are .NET-native). A note for vendor conversations: Pydantic also ships an add-on capabilities library coincidentally named "Pydantic AI Harness" — prebuilt components for code execution, guardrails, and sub-agent orchestration that the implementing team may adopt piecemeal. In this document, "the harness" always means Franklin's system, not that product. Tool layer: MCP Python SDK, containerized on Azure Container Apps. Input screening: Azure AI Content Safety plus custom filters. Observability: OpenTelemetry tracing of every agent step and tool call, with Pydantic Logfire or Azure Monitor as the sink — the trace and the audit log are separate records serving separate masters (engineering and compliance). Evaluations: a versioned suite of question-answer pairs with expected groundings, run in CI on every model, prompt, or schema change — Pydantic Evals is the natural companion to the recommended runtime, though promptfoo or a plain pytest structure serve equally well. Audit: append-only writes to immutable Azure storage, mirrored to Log Analytics for querying; the audit record includes user, role, question, tools called, records touched, and answer hash. Authorization rulebook: a versioned, machine-readable policy artifact in its own repository — Cedar or Open Policy Agent are both reasonable choices, and a reviewed YAML document with a shared evaluation library is enough to start — from which each server's rules are derived and against which each server is tested in CI. Relationship directory and person crosswalk: conformed tables in the lake, published into a small read-optimized store that tool servers query on their authorization path. Tool contracts: JSON Schema in a protocol-independent repository, with the MCP binding generated from it. FERPA posture: access under legitimate educational interest, demonstrated by the role-scoped enforcement and proven by the audit log; vendor data-processing agreements through the Azure/Foundry terms; zero-retention endpoints for any student-record traffic. One build-versus-buy question stays open by design: Azure's managed agent runtime inside Foundry could, in principle, replace the self-hosted orchestration container entirely, and the implementing team should formally evaluate it during phase one. The evaluation has a single acceptance test, and it is this document's non-negotiable: every tool call must execute under the individual user's identity, not the platform's service identity. Managed agent runtimes have historically failed exactly that test — tools run as the application, which is the confused-deputy design this architecture exists to prevent. If the managed service passes by the time we build, adopt it and operate one less container; if it does not, the self-hosted service has earned its keep, and we can say precisely why.

## 8. The security model, end to end

A skeptical reader should be able to trace one request and see where each protection sits, so here is the walk. A person signs in; Entra issues a short-lived token naming them and their roles. The router triages — helpfully, cheaply, and without authority. The execution model plans against only the tools that role can see; it holds no credentials and writes no free-form queries against institutional systems. Each tool call carries the user's token to an MCP server that independently re-validates it — the first enforcement point. The server's queries land on a lake whose row- and column-level rules check the same identity again — the second. The answer returns through output safeguards: small-cell suppression, required citations, and a groundedness check that compares the sources an answer cites against the servers a tool call actually reached. Every step lands in the audit log.

The design assumes four uncomfortable truths, three of them about attackers and one about ourselves. Language models can be manipulated, so no model's judgment is ever an enforcement mechanism — deceiving the router accomplishes nothing, because layers four and five do not listen to arguments. Data can be hostile — a Canvas discussion post is student-authored text that will eventually contain an instruction aimed at the AI reading it — so content-derived text is treated as data, never as instructions, and (in the read-write future) nothing derived from untrusted content flows into an action without human review. And aggregation leaks — "read-only" queries can re-identify students through small groups — so the suppression rules we already honor in federal reporting are enforced on every answer, uniformly, at the data layer where they cannot be bypassed.

Being precise about what this defeats matters as much as the defeating, because the strongest claim here is narrower than it first sounds. Because the tool server decides on the caller's own token, no amount of manipulating the model grants access to a record the person may not see: the confidentiality attack is closed structurally, and it is the one most people worry about. What remains open is integrity. Injected text cannot make the tool layer hand over a record; it can plausibly make an answer wrong in a targeted way — bias a summary, suppress a warning, describe a struggling student as on track — and for a system whose entire value is that somebody acts on what it says, that is a real harm with no structural defense. The partial answers are to keep student-authored text out of anything that decides, to quote it rather than summarize it, and to have answers say when a conclusion rests on free text rather than on structured records. Two related paths close at the surface and in the orchestrator respectively. An answer renders as inert text, with links displayed rather than fetched or auto-activated and no tool that retrieves an arbitrary address, so that hostile content cannot smuggle data out through the answer channel. And tool arguments come only from values a server itself returned, with the orchestrator checking each argument's provenance, so that hostile content cannot steer which record gets fetched even though it can never defeat the check on that record.

The audit log is the fourth uncomfortable truth, and it points the other way from the rest. It accumulates who asked what, about which student, when, and in which role — which makes it, in several respects, more sensitive than any single source it records, because it contains the questions. It is simultaneously the FERPA answer, a discovery exposure, and a record of staff and faculty behavior that was previously invisible. It therefore needs its own access control, a retention period set deliberately with counsel rather than by default, and alerting on volume and pattern rather than only recording them. The design correctly ensures that nobody sees more than they are entitled to; it just as correctly makes everything they are entitled to reachable in seconds, and that is a change in institutional risk even when every individual access is permitted.

Writes deserve their own sentences of restraint, and one distinction. Writes to course content — the kind CourseForge already performs when it pushes a course shell to Canvas — are low-sensitivity and reviewed by their nature, and they lead: this is where the write-tool pattern of typed tools, approval flow, and audit gets built and hardened, years before anything more sensitive depends on it. Writes to institutional records arrive last, one narrow tool at a time, each behind a propose-review-edit-enact workflow in which the human is the actor of record. In both cases the AI drafts; a person decides.

## 9. Building it: sequence, ownership, and the checklist

The build philosophy is spine first, organs one at a time. The spine — identity flow with scoped roles, gateway, audit, the authorization rulebook, the empty tool registry, the switchboard, and the lake's security scaffolding — must exist in version one even though version one connects to a single, harmless system, because these are the components no project successfully retrofits. Three more arrive before the first internal system connects rather than after it: the delegation model for unattended work (§5.3), the relationship directory and person crosswalk (§5.4, §5.5), and separated workload pools (§5.3). Each is a modest piece of engineering when built early and a retrofit across every server when built late. Every failed enterprise AI effort I can point to deferred authorization until after "proving value," then proved value on an architecture that could not support it. The scope of the build is also smaller than the word "platform" suggests: the agent loop, MCP plumbing, durable human-in-the-loop execution, and evaluation tooling now ship in mature open-source libraries, so the paid work — internal or contracted — is integration and governance code: identity exchange, the tool registry and its role filters, suppression, and audit. A proposal that prices the construction of an orchestration engine is pricing something that already exists, and should be read skeptically.

The rollout order follows sensitivity, ascending: first the IPEDS Oracle (already built, public data, zero FERPA exposure — it proves the whole pipeline with nothing at stake); second BLS/O\*NET and job postings (still external, and they buy a second constituency in career connectedness before any student record is touched); third Canvas (first internal system, with two running starts — the existing daily-digest work becomes the Canvas server's first read consumer, and CourseForge's course push becomes its first write consumer, on low-stakes content); fourth Colleague through the lake, read-only, after the security machinery has been exercised on lower-stakes data and the registrar has co-authored the definitions; Kuali wherever demand appears; record writes after everything, one tool at a time, with content writes arriving earlier through CourseForge as section 8 describes. Alongside phase one, CourseForge points its model calls at the institutional model plane — no functional change to the tool, immediate inheritance of institutional governance, and the first demonstration that the harness serves applications, not just chat.

On ownership: the vision does not require the visionary to build it, but it does require a named internal technical owner — a platform engineer in IT, or a designated lead if a consulting firm builds the spine. Named, and not alone: once advising depends on this, a single person who understands the whole system is a risk rather than a plan. The workable division is this: a consulting engagement can build the spine and the first two MCP servers against this document, with knowledge transfer as an explicit deliverable; internal IT owns operations, the onboarding checklist, and every subsequent server. Knowledge transfer delivered at the end of an engagement is the version that fails, and an internal engineer writing code inside the engagement from its first week is the version that works. What cannot be outsourced is stewardship — the definitions in the semantic layer and the authorization maps belong to Franklin employees (registrar, IR, IT security), because consultants leave and definitions stay.

Each new source comes under the umbrella through the same checklist, and the checklist is the governance story:

1. A named data steward has approved the server's authorization map — which roles reach which tools, scoped to which records.
2. The semantic definitions the source contributes are documented and, where applicable, reconciled to IPEDS definitions.
3. An evaluation set for the source's questions exists and passes before launch.
4. Suppression and sensitivity rules are reviewed for the new data.
5. Audit fields are confirmed flowing for every tool.

By the third system this checklist is a routine; by the fifth it is the reason a new source takes weeks rather than quarters.

## 10. Risks and honest objections

**"Why not wait for Ellucian?"** Ellucian's 2026 platform ships agentic AI inside its own walls, and for workflows that live entirely within Ellucian systems it may serve well. But its agents will never join our Canvas signals to our curriculum data to BLS projections to our IPEDS benchmarking, because no vendor federates sources it does not own. The harness treats Ellucian as one respected source among six. And no vendor platform will ever sit beneath an internally built tool the way the harness sits beneath CourseForge — the ability to power our own applications, not just answer questions, is exclusive to infrastructure we own. The honest framing: we are applying access-control architecture that regulated industries consider table stakes to a problem vendors would prefer to solve for us on their terms. Roughly four-fifths of this design is proven enterprise practice; most of the rest is current best practice that few institutions have assembled; the genuinely novel sliver is carrying per-user identity through agentic tool calls — which is precisely the part worth owning.

**Wrong answers.** The mitigations are structural: verified queries preferred over generated ones, a single semantic layer, mandatory citations, refusal when grounding fails, and the evaluation suite that catches quiet regressions when a model or schema changes. The system will still sometimes be wrong; the design goal is that it is wrong visibly, traceably, and correctably.

**Prompt injection and hostile content.** Addressed structurally in section 8; the summary is that no model's compliance is ever the thing standing between a user and data they should not see.

**Perception risk.** Faculty may hear "AI over Canvas data" as surveillance. The answer is candor and scope: the system answers questions people are already entitled to ask, faster, with better logging than the status quo — and the non-goals in section 3 should be communicated as prominently as the vision.

**Cost and drift.** The counterweights are the incremental sequence (each phase is separately fundable and separately stoppable), the checklist (which caps per-source cost), and the replaceable-runtime posture (which caps vendor exposure).

**The operating model, not the architecture.** The three failure modes most likely to end this are not technical, and they deserve to be stated as plainly as the technical ones. It ends if steward approval makes a new source take a quarter, because the entire economic argument is that it takes weeks — which is why the checklist in §9 needs a standing governance group with a published turnaround, delegated approval for low-sensitivity changes, and pre-approved patterns that need no new review at all ("aggregate, suppressed, program-scoped, read-only" should never queue). It ends if the platform owner leaves before there is a second one. And it ends if the governed path is slower and narrower than pasting into a browser tab, because then the institution has bought the risk and none of the control. That last failure makes latency and coverage governance properties rather than engineering preferences. A multi-source question realistically takes ten to thirty seconds against real upstreams — fine if the surface is designed for it, by showing the plan, showing tools as they return, and streaming the answer, and fatal if it presents as a blank box for twenty seconds. Publish a latency budget per class of question and treat a class that cannot meet it as a design defect rather than a fact of life.

## 11. Cost shape

Order-of-magnitude, for planning posture rather than budgeting: the spine plus the first two servers is a bounded build — a focused consulting engagement measured in a few hundred thousand dollars, or two to three internal quarters if IT can dedicate a platform engineer and fractional data engineering. The bound holds because the orchestration machinery is mature open source (section 9); the money buys integration and governance work, not engine construction. Two honesty adjustments belong here, and both came out of reviewing this document against enterprise deployment rather than against a pilot. The first is that the lake work — capacity, extracts, dbt models, row-level security design — is a project of comparable scale to the harness itself and should be a separate line rather than an assumption folded inside "spine plus two servers."

The second is that run costs need to be quoted per class of work rather than as one number, because this document proposes four. Model usage for people typing questions does bill in the low thousands per month at institutional scale, and it is self-limiting, because people only ask so much. Scheduled per-person analysis scales with headcount times frequency and accrues whether or not anybody opens the output; it is the class that can surprise a budget, and it is also the class with the weakest identity story (§5.3), which means the expensive thing and the risky thing are the same thing. Long-form generation scales with the course development calendar. Programmatic access scales with nothing at all, because a script has no natural limit — which is why per-person and per-surface quotas belong in the gateway from the start, and why a new consuming application should arrive with a budget owner attached rather than being quietly absorbed. Fabric capacity is likely an extension of commitments Franklin is already making, and the one genuine new recurring data line remains the licensed postings feed.

Staffing is the figure most likely to be wrong. "A fraction of a person" is right for a platform with two sources and no dependents. Six sources, a live user base, an evaluation suite, an onboarding checklist, and an on-call rotation is a full role plus fractional data engineering and fractional security — call it two to two and a half people once advising depends on it, and define the service level, the degraded mode, and the recovery plan before the first department is told to rely on it. A system like this is cheap to run and expensive only to neglect, and the cheapest form of neglect is staffing it for the pilot and never revisiting the number.

The return. The honest way to quantify return on infrastructure is break-even arithmetic, not projected percentages, so use conservative assumptions: a typical student completes about 60 credit hours with us at $398 per credit hour — roughly $24,000 in tuition revenue over their time at Franklin. Against a build measured in a few hundred thousand dollars, the entire spine-plus-first-servers investment is repaid by thirteen to seventeen additional retained students. Not per year — in total, over the system's life. Even discounting tuition heavily for instructional cost, break-even remains under three dozen students: a fraction of a percent of enrollment. The retention interventions in section 2 — an advisor learning which advisees have gone quiet this week, while there is still time to act — do not happen today because they are too expensive in staff time; this system makes them nearly free. No forecast is required. The only question is whether to bet against a number that small. One caveat, because a careful CFO will find it: the arithmetic assumes an attribution the system cannot claim on its own. The harness does not retain students; advisors acting on better information might, and gross tuition is not margin. The defensible version is to measure rather than assert — a controlled rollout with advisors who have the tool and advisors who do not, across one term — and the audit log already collects most of what that measurement needs. A break-even this small does not need a forecast, but it does deserve to be checked once.

The second return is capacity rather than cash: analysts and advisors currently spend real hours retrieving and reconciling data across systems before their actual work can begin. Governed self-service returns those hours to the work that requires their judgment — analysis, intervention, advising — and collapses turnaround from days to seconds.

The third is avoidance: vendor AI arrives as recurring platform uplifts, and ungoverned AI use of institutional data is already a sector-wide reality; owned, governed infrastructure substitutes for some of the former and insures against the latter.

The fourth return compounds. Today, every new AI use case at Franklin pays full price: its own model integration, its own credentials, its own security review, its own FERPA analysis, its own audit answer — or it quietly skips some of these, which is a different kind of expensive. The harness converts that from a recurring project into a checklist (section 9): the first system under the umbrella pays the fixed cost of the spine, and every system after pays only the marginal cost of one tool server and one steward review — quarters of effort becoming weeks. This is the return that grows with demand: AI use cases will keep arriving whether or not we build shared infrastructure; the harness only changes what each arrival costs, and what risks it carries in with it.

All of this is entitled to skepticism, which is why the system measures its own case: the audit log doubles as a usage record, and each phase is proposed against the observed use of the one before. No phase asks for faith in a forecast; each asks for a decision about evidence.

## 12. Decisions requested

This document asks for five things, because a vision with no decision attached gets admired and shelved. First, endorsement of the architecture's one non-negotiable — per-user identity end to end, enforced independently by the systems that own the data — as the standard any build, internal or contracted, must meet. Second, designation of data stewards for Colleague, Canvas, and Kuali data (registrar, IR, and academic affairs respectively is the natural map), together with a standing governance group and a published turnaround, so that stewardship is a process rather than a queue behind one person's calendar. Third, a named internal technical owner, with a decision on the internal-versus-contracted split for the spine. Fourth, authorization to proceed with phase one — the spine plus the IPEDS Oracle and labor-market servers — as a separately funded, separately evaluable step.

Fifth, and new to this revision: a decision in phase one on how unattended work acts for a person (§5.3). It is the one place where the non-negotiable will otherwise be excepted quietly rather than repealed openly, the decision is nearly free to make now, and it is expensive to reverse once a scheduled workload has been built on the wrong answer.

## 13. Glossary

- **Harness** — the institution-owned AI layer that connects people to systems; the umbrella, not any single tool.
- **CourseForge** — Franklin's internally built AI course-authoring tool, owned by instructional design; the first existing application to connect to the harness as a surface.
- **MCP (Model Context Protocol)** — an open standard for connecting AI systems to tools and data; each of our sources becomes one MCP server.
- **PEP (policy enforcement point)** — a place where authorization is independently checked; this design has two, the tool servers and the data platform. The front door triages and holds no authority.
- **Rulebook** — the single versioned document stating every access rule once; each enforcement point evaluates a cached copy, so the rules are written in one place and checked in several.
- **Scoped role** — a role plus the boundary it applies to: not "dean" but "dean of the College of Business."
- **Relationship directory** — the published facts that record-level rules turn on: which advisor is assigned to which student, which instructor teaches which section. Facts, not policy.
- **Individual grant** — one person's extra access, with a scope, a reason, an expiry, and a grantor; the alternative to inventing a role that nobody ever deletes.
- **Person crosswalk** — the mapping that says one human being in Colleague, Canvas, Kuali, and Entra is the same human being. A prerequisite for every cross-source answer.
- **Delegation register** — the explicit, revocable, time-boxed grants that let scheduled work act for a person who is not signed in.
- **Switchboard** — the ability to disable a tool, a source, a role, a surface, or one person immediately and without a deployment.
- **Token** — a short-lived, signed statement of who a user is and what roles they hold, issued at sign-in and carried through every layer.
- **Semantic layer** — the single place where institutional terms are defined as data logic, so "enrolled" means one thing everywhere.
- **Row-level security** — rules attached to data itself that filter what each identity can see, regardless of the application asking.
- **Small-cell suppression** — withholding aggregate results about very small groups so individuals cannot be identified; standard practice in federal reporting, applied here to every answer.
- **Entra ID** — Microsoft's identity platform, Franklin's existing sign-in.
- **Fabric / OneLake** — Microsoft's data platform, where the governed lake lives.
- **Foundry** — Microsoft's service for running commercial AI models (including Anthropic's and OpenAI's) inside an Azure environment under institutional governance.
- **Guardrails** — automated screening applied to requests coming in (hostile input) and answers going out (suppression, citations).
- **Audit log** — the append-only record of who asked what, when, under which role, touching which records.
